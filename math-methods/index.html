This post is the start of a review series on math methods that repeatedly show up in physics and electrical engineering. The goal is not to present every theorem in full generality, but to build a practical map of the tools that help us model systems, analyze measurements, and solve engineering problems.

This draft focuses only on one topic: the analytic signal. The analytic-signal view is central in communications, radar, controls, and physics-based signal analysis because it separates amplitude and phase behavior in a mathematically clean way.

Real signal and its analytic counterpart

Let $x(t)$ be a real-valued signal. Its analytic signal is defined as

$$ x_a(t) = x(t) + j\hat{x}(t), $$

where $\hat{x}(t)$ is the Hilbert transform of $x(t)$.

In the frequency domain,

$$ X_a(f) = 2U(f)X(f), $$

where $U(f)$ is the unit-step function in frequency:

$$ U(f) = \begin{cases} 1, & f > 0, \ \frac{1}{2}, & f = 0, \ 0, & f < 0. \end{cases} $$

So the analytic signal keeps only positive-frequency content (with DC handled by the half-weight convention).

Hilbert transform pair

The Hilbert transform is

$$ \hat{x}(t) = \mathcal{H}{x(t)} = \frac{1}{\pi},\mathrm{p.v.}!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{x(\tau)}{t-\tau},d\tau. $$

Equivalent frequency-domain form:

$$ \hat{X}(f) = -j,\operatorname{sgn}(f),X(f), $$

with

$$ \operatorname{sgn}(f) = \begin{cases} 1, & f > 0, \ 0, & f = 0, \ -1, & f < 0. \end{cases} $$

Hence

$$ X_a(f) = X(f) + j\hat{X}(f) = X(f) + \operatorname{sgn}(f)X(f) = 2U(f)X(f). $$

Magnitude and phase representation

Write the analytic signal in polar form:

$$ x_a(t) = A(t)e^{j\phi(t)}. $$

Then

$$ A(t) = |x_a(t)| = \sqrt{x^2(t) + \hat{x}^2(t)}, $$

and

$$ \phi(t) = \arg{x_a(t)}. $$

The original real signal is recovered by taking the real part:

$$ x(t) = \Re{x_a(t)} = A(t)\cos\phi(t). $$

Instantaneous frequency

When phase is unwrapped and differentiable, instantaneous frequency is

$$ f_i(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d\phi(t)}{dt}. $$

This is useful for nonstationary and narrowband signals where local frequency content evolves over time.

Connection to complex envelope

For a bandpass signal centered near $f_c$, define

$$ x_a(t) = \tilde{x}(t)e^{j2\pi f_c t}, $$

where $\tilde{x}(t)$ is the complex envelope (complex baseband). Therefore,

$$ ilde{x}(t) = x_a(t)e^{-j2\pi f_c t}. $$

If we write

$$ ilde{x}(t)=x_I(t)+jx_Q(t), $$

then the real passband signal can be written as

$$ x(t)=x_I(t)\cos(2\pi f_c t)-x_Q(t)\sin(2\pi f_c t). $$

This decomposition is the standard I/Q representation used throughout communication and radar systems.

Short derivation for a cosine

Let

$$ x(t)=\cos(2\pi f_0 t). $$

Its Hilbert transform is

$$ \hat{x}(t)=\sin(2\pi f_0 t). $$

Therefore,

$$ x_a(t)=\cos(2\pi f_0 t)+j\sin(2\pi f_0 t)=e^{j2\pi f_0 t}, $$

which has only a positive-frequency spectral line. This is the simplest example showing why the analytic signal is called analytic in frequency construction.

Practical notes

  1. The analytic-signal interpretation is strongest for narrowband or monocomponent signals.
  2. For multicomponent signals, instantaneous phase and frequency can become ambiguous.
  3. In sampled systems, Hilbert transforms are approximated with FIR/IIR filters or FFT-domain constructions.
  4. Edge effects and phase unwrapping errors can bias envelope and instantaneous-frequency estimates.
