Observing Satellites

How to predicting where to find satellites in the night sky.


Oct 8, 2025 Orbital Dynamics Satellites

Observing satellites in the night sky can be a fun way to learn about orbital dynamics and learn your way around the earth and sky. This discussion is specific to observers in the northern hemisphere since I am currently living in the Rocky Mountain West area.

First, let’s review some important terminology:

Azimuth is the horizontal angle, measured in degrees, from true north in a clockwise direction. The star Polaris lies within one degree of true north and is therefore a useful reference point for determining azimuth in the night sky. To locate Polaris, first find the Big Dipper (Ursa Major) and identify the two stars that form the far edge of the dipper’s cup. Extending an imaginary line through these stars about five times their spacing will point to Polaris, a magnitude~+2 star. Because of the geometry of Earth’s rotation, Polaris appears at an altitude approximately equal to the observer’s latitude.

Altitude is the angular distance measured in degrees above the local horizon. Generally, altitude can be a little tricky to estimate by eye due to terrain and the fact that people often underestimate altitude the higher the the object is in the sky.

Coordinates are the latitude and longitude of your observing location on earth. You can look these up online, or use a GPS receiver to estimate them.

Time the local time of your observation window in a standard time format (e.g., UTC)

One of the best websites for predicting when you can observe satellites is https://heavens-above.com. After entering your location, you can browse upcoming passes filtered by brightness (magnitude), azimuth, and elevation. The site also provides detailed pass information for each satellite, including when and where it will appear in the sky, its maximum altitude, and the path it will follow overhead. Viewability - Brightness using Magnitude System

Astronomers measure the brightness of stars using the magnitude scale. Lower or negative numbers mean brighter objects.

For reference:

  • The Sun is about –27 mag,
  • The full Moon about –12.5 mag,
  • Sirius (the brightest nighttime star) about –1.5 mag,
  • And the faintest stars visible to the unaided eye under dark skies are around +6 mag.

Knowing a few benchmark magnitudes is helpful when estimating how bright a satellite will appear, and for judging how good your viewing conditions are. In the Northern Hemisphere, Ursa Minor is a convenient guide because it is circumpolar and contains stars ranging from about +2 to +6 mag. Optical equipment lets you see much fainter objects. A good 50 mm binocular can extend visibility from about +6 mag to around +10 mag. A 6–8 inch reflector telescope can reach roughly +14 mag, although its smaller field of view makes tracking more challenging. Following a satellite through a telescope typically requires a motorized mount and accurate orbital data. Even with current USSPACECOM tracking elements, pointing errors of up to one degree are common.
