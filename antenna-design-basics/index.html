Antenna Design Basics

Choosing an antenna for your RF project


Nov 16, 2025 RF Antenna Communications

If you plan to dive into a Radio Frequency (RF) project, getting familiar with a few core antenna types is essential. Choosing or designing the right antenna is one of the most fundamental skills in electrical engineering, and understanding the first principles behind them will help you move quickly from concept to a working system that transmits and receives reliably.

Parabolic Dish:

The dish diameter $D$ determines the gain.

$$ G = \eta (\pi D / \lambda)^2 ,$$

where $\eta$ is the antenna efficiency (typically 0.45 - .70). Note that each doubling of the dish diameter increases the gain by $6dB$.

The beamwidth for a given frequency (or wavelength) is also determined by the diameter.

$$ \text{Beamwidth} \approx \frac{70\lambda}{D}$$

The dish will work for any frequency where $D \gg \lambda$. The surface error also needs to be good enough (very important about 10 GHz).

$$ \text{RMS surface error} \le \lambda/16$$

The required $G/T$ (i.e., link budget) determines the gain needed. For example, if you want to receive an L-band signal at 1680 MHz from the weather satellite GOES-18 in geostationary orbit, you might want 24 dBi of gain from the antenna.

$$ D = \frac{\lambda}{\pi} 10^\frac{24 - 10\log_{10}(\eta)}{20}$$

Using $\lambda = 0.178$ m, $\eta = 0.6$.

$$ D \approx 0.68 \text{m} .$$

It is also important to know where to place the feed. For a direct focus configuration, the focal length is

$$ F = \frac{D^2}{16c}$$,

where $F$ is the focal length and $c$ is the depth measured from the center of the dish to the height equal to the outer rim.

Possibly the single most important geometric parameter of a dish antenna is the $F/D$ (focal length divided by the diameter). This determines the design of the feed.

To understand this, it is helpful to think of the feed transmitting a beam towards the dish. The feed illumination pattern must match the dish geometry. If it is too wide, the beam spills over the edge and the result is a higher system noise temperature (LNA sees noise from ground). If it is too narrow (“under-illumination”) the result is lower gain. The type of feed is tied directly to $F/D$. You can look up more information about this, but it will not be covered here.

Let’s look at an example like the Discovery Dish used for ameature L-band weather satellite reception. The dish diameter is 70 cm and the depth is 12 cm. So $F/D = 0.36$. The half illumination angle is

$$ \theta = \arctan\left(\frac{D/2}{F}\right) = 53.6^\circ $$

and the full illumination is $2\theta \approx 107^\circ $. Typically, we want the spillover to be less than 5%, so we could choose a feed with a beamwidth of approximately 105 degrees at 1680 MHz. That is very wide, so a patch, helical feed or simple PCB feed would work well. However, when choosing a feed, keep in mind that spillover is the most common source of noise after the LNA noise figure. This is due to the sky temperature being approximately 3 Kelvin and the warm ground being approximately 280 Kelvin. Significant spillover can actually add more noise than your LNA noise figure in L-band.

For receiveing weak signals, everything comes down to sensitivity.

$$ \text{Sensitivity} = \frac{G}{T_{system}} $$
