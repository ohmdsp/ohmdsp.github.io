The Analytic Signal

Understand the analytic signal for both EE and Physics


Aug 1, 2026 Math Methods Analytic Signal Hilbert Transform DSP

This post focuses on the analytic signal. The analytic-signal view is central in communications, radar, controls, and physics-based signal analysis because it separates amplitude and phase behavior in a mathematically clean way. The word “analytic” here is not a coincidence of terminology — it’s the same complex-analysis concept from a math methods course, applied on the time axis instead of the spatial or complex-variable axis it’s usually introduced with. This post builds the signal-processing machinery first, then closes the loop back to complex analysis to show why the name fits.

What “analytic” means, briefly

A function $\omega(z)$ is analytic in a region if it has a derivative at every point in that region, where the derivative

$$ \frac{d\omega}{dz} = \lim_{\Delta z \to 0} \frac{\omega(z+\Delta z)-\omega(z)}{\Delta z} $$

must be independent of the direction $\Delta z$ approaches zero from. Writing $\omega(z) = u(x,y) + i v(x,y)$, this path-independence is equivalent to the Cauchy-Riemann equations:

$$ \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} \qquad\qquad \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} $$

In other words: $u$ and $v$ aren’t independent real functions once the analytic condition is imposed — each one determines the other (up to a constant), and they’re called harmonic conjugates. Keep that pairing in mind; it’s exactly what the Hilbert transform produces below.

Real-valued signal and its analytic counterpart

Let $x(t)$ be a real-valued signal. We can form the analytic signal $s(t) = x(t) + j\hat{x}(t)$, where $\hat{x}(t)$ is the Hilbert transform of $x(t)$. Its Fourier transform $S(\omega)$ is zero for $\omega < 0$ by construction.

$$ x_a(t) = x(t) + j\hat{x}(t). $$

In the frequency domain,

$$ X_a(f) = \begin{cases} 2X(f), & f > 0, \\ 0, & f \le 0. \end{cases} $$

So the analytic signal keeps only positive-frequency content (one-sided spectrum). This one-sidedness is often how we define an analytic signal in signal processing courses and papers.

Hilbert transform pair

The Hilbert transform is

$$ \hat{x}(t) = \mathcal{H}\{x(t)\} = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{x(\tau)}{t-\tau}\,d\tau. $$

This integral has a problem: the denominator $t-\tau$ hits zero when $\tau = t$, so the integrand blows up right at the point we’re integrating through. To make sense of the integral, we exclude a small symmetric window of width $2\epsilon$ around $\tau = t$ and shrink it to zero after integrating:

$$ \hat{x}(t) = \frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\epsilon \to 0^+} \left(\int_{-\infty}^{t-\epsilon} + \int_{t+\epsilon}^{\infty}\right) \frac{x(\tau)}{t-\tau}\,d\tau. $$

Because the window is symmetric, the parts of the integral that blow up on either side of $\tau=t$ cancel each other out, leaving a finite result. In practice this rarely needs to be handled explicitly — discrete implementations (FIR/IIR Hilbert filters, or the FFT-domain construction used later in this post) never evaluate the kernel exactly at $\tau=t$, so the singularity doesn’t show up numerically in practice.

The frequency-domain form follows directly, since the integral above is a convolution and convolution becomes multiplication in frequency:

$$ \hat{X}(f) = -j\,\operatorname{sgn}(f)\,X(f), $$

with

$$ \operatorname{sgn}(f) = \begin{cases} 1, & f > 0, \\ 0, & f = 0, \\ -1, & f < 0. \end{cases} $$

So the Hilbert transform is just a filter, $H(f) = -j,\operatorname{sgn}(f)$, that leaves magnitude alone and shifts phase by $-90°$ at positive frequencies and $+90°$ at negative frequencies — nothing more exotic than that.

$$ X_a(f) = X(f) + j\hat{X}(f) = X(f) + \operatorname{sgn}(f)X(f). $$

Here’s why that combination produces a one-sided spectrum: adding $j\hat{X}(f)$ to $X(f)$ turns the $-90°$ phase shift on the positive side into constructive addition, doubling those components ($X(f)+X(f)=2X(f)$ for $f>0$), while the $+90°$ shift on the negative side produces destructive cancellation, zeroing them out entirely ($X(f)-X(f)=0$ for $f<0$). At $f=0$, $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0$, so the component is left unchanged. The result is exactly the one-sided spectrum introduced earlier — the original positive-frequency content scaled by 2, and nothing at negative frequencies.

Magnitude and phase representation

Let’s write the analytic signal in polar form:

$$ x_a(t) = A(t)e^{j\phi(t)}, $$

where

$$ A(t) = |x_a(t)| = \sqrt{x^2(t) + \hat{x}^2(t)}, $$

and

$$ \phi(t) = \arg\{x_a(t)\}. $$

The original real signal is recovered by taking the real part:

$$ x(t) = \Re\{x_a(t)\} = A(t)\cos\phi(t). $$

Instantaneous frequency

When phase is unwrapped and differentiable, instantaneous frequency is

$$ f_i(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d\phi(t)}{dt}. $$

This is useful for nonstationary and narrowband signals where local frequency content evolves over time.

Connection to complex envelope

For a bandpass signal centered near $f_c$, define

$$ x_a(t) = \tilde{x}(t)e^{j2\pi f_c t}, $$

where $\tilde{x}(t)$ is the complex envelope (complex baseband). Therefore,

$$ \tilde{x}(t) = x_a(t)e^{-j2\pi f_c t}. $$

If we write

$$ \tilde{x}(t)=x_I(t)+jx_Q(t), $$

then the real passband signal can be written as

$$ x(t)=x_I(t)\cos(2\pi f_c t)-x_Q(t)\sin(2\pi f_c t). $$

This decomposition is the standard I/Q representation used throughout communication and radar systems.

Short derivation for a cosine

Let

$$ x(t)=\cos(2\pi f_0 t). $$

Its Hilbert transform is

$$ \hat{x}(t)=\sin(2\pi f_0 t). $$

Therefore,

$$ x_a(t)=\cos(2\pi f_0 t)+j\sin(2\pi f_0 t)=e^{j2\pi f_0 t}, $$

which has only a positive-frequency spectral line.

This example is small enough to check the analytic condition directly. Extend $t$ to a complex variable $z = t + j\tau$ and evaluate the same formula there:

$$ x_a(z) = e^{j2\pi f_0 z} = e^{j2\pi f_0 t}\,e^{-2\pi f_0 \tau}. $$

This is entire (analytic everywhere) in $z$, and for $\tau > 0$ it decays rather than blows up — well-behaved in the upper half of the complex $t$-plane. That decay for $\tau>0$ is not a coincidence of this particular example; it’s forced by $f_0 > 0$, i.e., by the one-sided spectrum. The general case below shows why.

Why the analytic signal is actually analytic

Go back to the single-tone case worked out above: $x_a(t) = e^{j2\pi f_0 t}$ for a positive frequency $f_0$. Extending $t$ into the complex plane as $z = t + j\tau$ gave

$$ x_a(z) = e^{j2\pi f_0 t}\,e^{-2\pi f_0 \tau}, $$

an oscillation at $f_0$ multiplied by a decaying exponential in $\tau$. That decay only happens because $f_0 > 0$. If $f_0$ were negative, the same substitution would give a term that grows without bound as $\tau$ increases.

A general analytic signal is a sum of tones like this one, weighted by $X_a(f)$, but restricted entirely to $f>0$. Tilt time into the complex plane the same way, and every one of those tones picks up a decaying factor $e^{-2\pi f\tau}$ for $\tau>0$ — none of them grow, because none of them have negative frequency. Stack up infinitely many decaying pieces and the sum stays well-behaved: it converges, and it stays smooth (differentiable) as $z$ moves around in that region. That’s the entire content of “analytic” from earlier in this post, arrived at using a physical explanation that nothing in the signal has an ingredient that blows up in that direction.

If even one negative-frequency component were present, its exponential would grow instead of decay, and the sum would diverge and the function would fail to be analytic there. So restricting to $f>0$ is precisely the condition that keeps the complex-time extension finite. $x_a(t)$ is the boundary value of that extension as $\tau \to 0^+$ — the signal you actually measure is the edge of a better-behaved function living just off the real time axis.

This should look familiar

It’s the same logic as the region of convergence (ROC) for a one-sided signal’s Laplace or Z-transform: a causal signal ($x(t)=0$ for $t<0$, one-sided in time) has a transform that converges, and is therefore analytic, only in a half-plane of the complex frequency variable $s$ (or outside some radius in $z$). Here the one-sidedness is in frequency instead of time, so the roles swap: a one-sided spectrum forces the analytic condition in a half-plane of complex time. Same convergence mechanism, opposite domain.

Tying $x$ and $\hat{x}$ together

On the real axis ($\tau=0$), $x_a(t) = x(t) + j\hat{x}(t)$ - the in-phase/quadrature signal. But because $x_a(z)$ is analytic, its real and imaginary parts aren’t free to be anything: the Cauchy-Riemann equations lock them into a specific pair (harmonic conjugates), and on the real axis that constraint is exactly the Hilbert transform relation between $x$ and $\hat{x}$. The Hilbert transform is the transform that falls out once you insist the spectrum is one-sided.

And the loop closes with causality

Run the identical argument with time and frequency swapped: a causal impulse response $h(t)$ (zero for $t<0$) has a Fourier transform $H(\omega)$ that is analytic in the upper half of the complex $\omega$-plane, for exactly the same convergence reason. That analytic condition forces the real and imaginary parts of $H(\omega)$ into a Hilbert-transform pair of their own: the Kramers-Kronig relations. The analytic signal is the mirror image of a causal system and is one-sided in frequency instead of time, analytic in time instead of frequency.
