My First Startup Experience

Joining Network Elements Inc. as employee 21 and learning what startup life was like


Jul 31, 2022 Startup Career Physics Engineering First Job

After finishing my BSc in Physics, my first job was at Network Elements Inc (NEI). in Beaverton, Oregon. Network Elements was a technology company focused on networking solutions and 10Gb/s and 40GB/s optical components. I joined as employee #21 and the first new grad hire on the team. That made the experience memorable from the beginning: I was walking into a small company where nearly everyone around me had already built a significant amount of technical and professional experience. The three founders and most the early team were from Tektronix and had many years of design and development experience.

This post is a reflection on what it was like to interview, start, and work through that first couple years. It was my first close look at how a startup actually operates, the fund raising cycles, how much responsibility can land on a young engineer very quickly, and how much you can learn when you are surrounded by capable people building real technology.

Interviewing at Network Elements

Coming out of a physics program, my original plan was to continue to study physics in graduate school. However, a professor in the ECE department that taught the laser design courses I took recommended me to NEI when they contacted the ECE department looking for a student that had experience in optics and lasers. I was excited about NEI since they were working on state-of-the-art technology related to optoelectronics. It seemed like a place where I could get experience and mentoring that would not be available in graduate school.

The interview process was an all day event. I drove from Corvallis to Beaverton early in the morning to meet at the NEI office. They had brought in seven candidates from various universities around the country. Several of the other candidates were computer science or electrical engineering majors. I was the only physics major. After meeting with the HR director and having donuts, we gathered in a large conference room along with additional memebers of the NEI founding and technical team. Each of the candidates took turns giving a 30 to 40 minute technical presentation. I recall that it was very interesting to hear the presentations from other candidates. I gave a presentation on my lab work in laser colling and atom trapping.

After the presentations we had lunch and met a few more NEI employees. Then we broke out into 1-on-1 interviews. If my memory is correct, we had four 1-hour interviews in the afternoon. For the most part, I really enjoyed these interviews. It gave me a chance to meet the NEI founding team and learn more about what they were working on. It was also a fun challenge to be asked a lot of EE questions and learn that I could answer them due to my experience working in a lab building and troubleshooting laser temperature and current control circuitry.

After the afternoon interviews, we all went to dinner. Again, a handful of the NEI founding team came along too. After dinner, we played indoor laser tag at a commercial facility in the area. This was a fun way to unwind from the day of interviews.

I do not think it is common these days to hold all day interviewing events like companies did back in the early 2000s. Although it is time consuming, it really is a good way to get to know eachother and get a sense of what kind of people you might be working with. After a few days, I received a call and a job offer was extended to me. Looking back at the photos, I can say that they extended an offer to three of the seven that interviewed. I was the first one to start work in early Jan, 2000.

Starting as employee #21

Being employee #21 meant the company was still small enough that the culture felt highly personal. People knew what others were working on, decisions had visible consequences, and there was a sense that everyone was helping shape the direction of the company in real time.

As the first new college graduate hire, I also felt the gap between school and industry immediately. A physics degree gave me useful fundamentals, but it did not automatically teach me how products get built, how schedules drive decisions, how teams communicate on complex projects, or how much practical knowledge lives in the experience of other engineers.

I shared an office with the Chief Engineer, Phil Crosby. Phil was an amazing engineer and mentor. He would get the young engineers involved in everything, and make sure they were working on real and meaningful problems. Two other people deserve special mention also: Ed Hershberg and Richard Booman. Like Phil, they were both amazing engineers and fantastic mentors. That first stretch was both exciting and humbling. I had to learn quickly, ask better questions, and get comfortable taking ownership of important problems. In hindsight, that was one of the most valuable parts of the experience. The culture at NEI in the early years was incredible for a young engineer. We learned, we solved problems, we generated products and patents, and we made great friends.

VC Funding

The first year of a startup job can compress a lot of learning into a short amount of time. At Network Elements, I began to understand that engineering in industry is not just about technical correctness. It is also about priorities, timing, tradeoffs, communication, and execution.

Several lessons from that first year stayed with me:

  1. Strong fundamentals matter, but they are only the starting point.
  2. Experienced teammates can accelerate your growth if you pay attention and ask good questions.
  3. Real engineering work is shaped by constraints, not just elegant theory.
  4. Small companies give you a clearer view of how business and technology interact.
  5. Your attitude, curiosity, and reliability matter as much as raw technical ability.

I also learned how energizing it can be to work in a place where technology is being actively built and refined rather than simply maintained. Even when the pace is demanding, it is rewarding to feel close to the work that matters.

The people made the experience

One of the best parts of that first year was getting to work alongside people who had already done hard things. At that stage in my career, I benefited enormously from being around engineers and colleagues who had more experience than I did and who carried practical knowledge that cannot be learned from textbooks alone.

Looking back, I do not just remember the projects or the office. I remember the people, the tone of the workplace, the conversations, and the example that others set. Your first job teaches you technical skills, but it also teaches you what professional excellence looks like in everyday work.

Technology that made the startup feel real

Another part of what made the experience memorable was getting to see actual technology being developed inside a small company. Early in your career, there is a big difference between studying ideas in class and seeing hardware, systems, and products take shape in a real engineering environment.

That was part of what made startup life feel so immediate. The technology was not abstract. It had schedules, customers, constraints, and people responsible for making it work.

Looking back

My first year at Network Elements gave me something I could not have gotten from school alone: a direct introduction to the pace, uncertainty, and opportunity of real engineering work inside a well-funded startup. It taught me how much there is to learn beyond theory, how valuable experienced teammates can be, and how formative a first job can be when you enter an environment that expects you to grow quickly.

I still think of that experience as an important part of my life. It was where I began to see how technical work, business goals, and team culture all come together. For a first job after a physics degree, it was an excellent place to start.
