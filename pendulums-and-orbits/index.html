Pendulums and Orbits

Relating pendulum dynamics to orbital period and satellite look-angle geometry


Jul 1, 2026 Physics Orbits Mechanics

In this technical post, we derive the relationship between the period of an orbit and the radius of the orbit using a simple pendulum. Then we look at how to compute azimuth and elevation angles to a satellite using orbital parameters.

Introduction

It took Kepler sixteen years to derive his third law. However, given Newton’s laws, we can derive the relationship between the period of an orbit and the radius of the orbit using a simple pendulum. If we consider the period of a pendulum whose length is equal to the radius of the Earth $R_e$, we can derive a relationship between the period of an orbit and the radius of the orbit. This period has an important meaning, and we will see that it is also possible to consider other satellite orbits using the relationship derived.

The Pendulum

Newton’s second law tells us that the force experienced by a body is equal to its mass times its acceleration. In the case of the pendulum in Figure 1, we can write the force component due to gravity $mg$ in the direction of acceleration as

$$ F = mg\sin\theta $$

which for small angles can be approximated as

$$ F \approx mg\theta. $$

The tangential acceleration of the rotating mass at the end of a massless rod is

$$ a = L\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}. $$

Combining these equations gives

$$ -mg\sin\theta = mL\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} $$

which can be rearranged to

$$ \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} + \frac{g}{L}\sin\theta = 0. $$

A solution to this differential equation is

$$ \theta(t) = \theta_0\cos(\omega t) $$

where

$$ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} $$

is the angular frequency. The period of the pendulum can be computed by

$$ T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}. $$

If we assume the rod length is equal to the radius of the Earth, or $L=6358$ kilometers, and $g=9.8$ meters per second squared, the period is approximately $5,061$ seconds, or about $84.4$ minutes. This is known as the Schuler period, and is also the orbital period of a satellite orbiting a spherical Earth of radius $L=6358$ kilometers at sea level. If we increase $L$, the orbital period gets longer.

Satellites

Up to this point, we used $g=9.8\ \frac{m}{s^2}$ for acceleration due to gravity. However, this is only true at Earth’s surface and changes for larger orbit distances. Using Newton’s full expression for gravitational force between two objects:

$$ F = \frac{GM_sM_e}{R_e^2} $$

where $G$ is the gravitational constant and $M_e$ and $M_s$ are the masses of the Earth and satellite. Notice that $g$ is shorthand for $\frac{GM_e}{R_e^2}$.

We can now write the period of an orbit at radius $L$ from the center of a spherical Earth as

$$ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{R_e}{\frac{GM_e}{R_e^2}}}. $$

This shows the relationship between period and radius:

$$ T \propto R_e^3 $$

which is Kepler’s third law.

As an additional example, if we want a period of $24$ hours ($86,400$ seconds), we can solve for the pendulum length. The result is approximately $42,250$ kilometers, which is near the radius of a geostationary orbit (GEO) in the equatorial plane.

Orbital Parameters

Elliptical orbits are typically defined using six Keplerian parameters. The first three are:

  1. Semi-major axis $a$
  2. Eccentricity $e$ (with $e=0$ for a circular orbit)
  3. Time $t_0$ at which the object is at a defined orbital position (for example, time of perigee)

These three define the orbit in the two-dimensional orbital plane.

The remaining parameters place that orbit in three-dimensional space:

  1. Inclination $i$
  2. Argument of perigee $\omega$
  3. Right ascension $\Omega$

Satellite Relative Position

Given an Earth-station geodetic position (latitude/longitude), it is useful to compute the relative position of a satellite, star, or other object in terms of angles and distance. For example, the following two-step process can be used for a GPS satellite.

Step 1: compute the mean anomaly $M$ as

$$ M = M_0 + \frac{d\omega}{dt}(t-t_0) $$

where $M_0$ is the mean anomaly at a reference time $t_0$ (epoch), and $\frac{d\omega}{dt}$ is the angular velocity.

Then solve Kepler’s equation for eccentric anomaly $E$:

$$ M = E - e\sin E $$

This equation is nonlinear in $E$, so it is solved iteratively (for example with Newton’s method).

The position of the satellite in the orbital plane is

$$ x_0 = a(\cos E - e) $$$$ y_0 = a(1-e^2)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sin E $$$$ r = (x_0^2 + y_0^2)^{\frac{1}{2}} $$

where $a$ is the semi-major axis length.

Step 2: use inclination $i$, right ascension $\Omega$, and argument of perigee $\omega$ to transform from the perifocal coordinate system to the geocentric coordinate system:

$$ P_x = \cos\omega\cos\Omega - \sin\omega\sin\Omega\cos i $$$$ P_y = \cos\omega\sin\Omega + \sin\omega\cos\Omega\cos i $$$$ P_z = \sin\omega\sin i $$$$ Q_x = -\sin\omega\cos\Omega - \cos\omega\sin\Omega\cos i $$$$ Q_y = -\sin\omega\sin\Omega + \cos\omega\cos\Omega\cos i $$$$ Q_z = \cos\omega\sin i $$

The satellite position in geocentric coordinates is then

$$ x = P_xx_0 + Q_xy_0 $$$$ y = P_yx_0 + Q_yy_0 $$$$ z = P_zx_0 + Q_zy_0 $$

Finally, the following equations can be used to compute satellite azimuth and elevation from a specified Earth station:

$$ \text{Right Ascension},\ \alpha = \arctan(y/x) $$$$ \text{Declination},\ \delta = \arctan\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\right) $$$$ \text{Elevation},\ \eta = \arctan\left(\frac{\sin\eta_s - \frac{R}{r}}{\cos\eta_s}\right) $$

with

$$ \eta_s = \arcsin\left[\sin\delta\sin\theta_e + \cos\delta\cos\theta_e\cos\phi_{se}\right]. $$

where:

  • $R$: Earth radius
  • $r$: satellite distance from Earth center
  • $\theta_e$: earth station latitude
  • $\phi_{se} = \phi_s - \phi_e$
  • $\phi_s$: satellite longitude
  • $\phi_e$: earth station longitude

Azimuth can be computed by

$$ A = \arctan\left[\frac{\sin\phi_{se}}{\cos\theta_e\tan\delta - \sin\theta_e\cos\phi_{se}}\right] $$

and the slant range from Earth station to satellite is

$$ \rho = \sqrt{r^2 - R^2\cos^2\eta} - R\sin\eta. $$

There are important correction rules for northern and southern hemispheres, and for whether the satellite lies east or west of the Earth station. In practice, these are quadrant corrections for azimuth. Those corrections are implemented in the MATLAB code included in the appendix.

Conclusion

We related the period of a pendulum with a very long rod length $L$ to the period of a satellite orbit with radius $L$. Then we outlined how to compute satellite azimuth and elevation from a known earth station location.
