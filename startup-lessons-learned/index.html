Startup Lessons Learned

Reflections from 8 years leading KickView Corporation


Jul 16, 2024 Startup Leadership Entrepreneurship CEO Lessons Learned

This post curates my lessons learned while building and running a small tech startup from 2014 to 2022. During that time, I kept a journal of the most important lessons I learned from starting and serving as CEO of KickView Corporation for 8 years. It was, without question, a challenging and rewarding period of my career. Hopefully, others can benefit from the lessons I learned along the way.

The most important lesson

One of the best things about doing a startup is the people - the ones you work with and the ones you meet along the way. I would say the experience helped me connect with people in ways that were not possible while I was an engineer at an established company. When you are the founder of a startup, you quickly learn the value of a wide variety of skills needed for running a business - accounting, legal services, commercial real estate, janitorial services, construction and building maintenance, administration, marketing, business development, etc. There are so many talents and skills needed to make the business world function, and engineering is only one small piece of that apparatus. It was humbling (in a good way) to meet so many talented and hard-working people adding value through their various skills and domain expertise. So, my first lesson learned (and most important) is to stay humble, appreciate and respect other people, and embrace the opportunity to interact with and learn from others.

Business for engineering dummies

The next most important lesson is learn about basic business finance and accounting! Ideally, you want to do some of that work prior to starting a business, but it is also a fact that you can only learn some things by actually doing them. Similar to learning how to parent, your startup will be unique enough from other businesses that you will need to do some “learning on the job” in order to master it. However, that does not mean ignoring the basics. So, you will need to understand how to do some basic accounting, forecasting, and how to create and understand a balance sheet, profit-and-loss statement, and cap table (if you are raising money). I was surprised by how creative the financial aspects of the job turned out to be. It was actually more like engineering than I expected. You get to create processes and data analysis methods that work best for your business. Embrace it!

Lessons of credibility

I am proud of the fact that KickView started from scratch and was profitable every year after the first year, with revenue increasing each year. After the second year, it became a multi-million dollar per year business, and we never experienced a year without significant revenue increase. The reason I mention this is that revenue is something that can be measured and correlated with business progress. It is by no means the only metric that can be measured, and I would also say it is short-sighted to say it defines success. I use it here only to provide some credibility to the lessons I want to tell you about next.

Who is who in the zoo?

I am sure this phrase “who is who in the zoo” can be attributed to someone else, but I first heard it from a mentor. When I started KickView, I engaged with an attorney at a large firm in Boulder, Colorado to help with setting up the corporate documents. After the documents were in place, I asked my corporate attorney if she would introduce me to a handful of entrepreneurs in the area that had started tech companies. My goal was twofold: find a mentor, and find some potential investors.

I was introduced to several people by my attorney, and one person became both a mentor and angel investor. This relationship also turned into a friendship that lasts to this day. That mentor told me that there are four buyers in any transaction: the mentor, the financial buyer, the technical buyer, and the user buyer. He told me that it was very important to identify these buyers as soon as possible when initiating a relationship with other customers or organizations. I won’t go into details about this here, but I found through experience that this is actually true. When I could identify these buyers (actual individuals), business interactions and deals went more smoothly. My next lesson learned builds on this concept: the financial buyer and the user buyer are the most important. Do not waste all your time talking to the engineering buyer - he/she most likely wants to build or offer what you are trying to sell.

What is cultural fit?

You will hear the term cultural fit mentioned a lot when talking about hiring for a startup. What does it actually mean? I will tell you that it does not mean that a hire should be interested in the same things, have similar skills, or be someone you want to be friends with. In fact, you should try to hire people that challenge your ideas and skill level regardless of how fun they are to hang out with. What you do not want is someone that has a huge ego, no humility and cannot suffer being challenged themselves. Don’t hire someone that is toxic to others just because they are brilliant. It will not be worth it.

The journey is the destination

It can be easy to spend all your time thinking about where you want to be in 6 months, a year, 2 years, etc., and forget to focus on the now. When it comes to building a company, a team, and a technology, the journey can be very rewarding and often a lot of fun. Although headlines like to talk about the VC raise, the acquisition, the IPO, and revenue projections, when you talk to founders in person they love to talk about the experiences, the struggles, and the hard work along the way. Life is most enjoyable when we have unique experiences with interesting people. That can be accomplished on journeys that end with a wide range of outcomes.
