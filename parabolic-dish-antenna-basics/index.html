Parabolic Dish Antenna Basics

Choosing the right antenna for your RF project


Nov 16, 2025 RF Antenna Satcom

If you plan to dive into a Radio Frequency (RF) project, getting familiar with a few core antenna types is essential. Choosing or designing the right antenna is one of the most fundamental skills in electrical engineering, and understanding the first principles behind them will help you move quickly from concept to a working system that transmits and receives reliably.

Parabolic Dish:

The dish diameter $D$ determines the gain.

$$ G = \eta (\pi D / \lambda)^2 ,$$

where $\eta$ is the antenna efficiency (typically 0.45 - .70). Each doubling of the dish diameter increases the gain by $6dB$.

The beamwidth for a given frequency (or wavelength) is also determined by the diameter.

$$ \text{Beamwidth} \approx \frac{70\lambda}{D}$$

The dish will work effectively for any frequency where $D \gg \lambda$. The surface accuracy also matters, especially above 10 GHz:

$$ \text{RMS surface error} \le \lambda/16$$

The required $G/T$ (from your link budget) determines the gain needed. For example, if you want to receive an L-band signal at 1680 MHz from the GOES-18 weather satellite in geostationary orbit, you might want around 24 dBi of antenna gain.

$$ D = \frac{\lambda}{\pi} 10^\frac{24 - 10\log_{10}(\eta)}{20}$$

Using $\lambda = 0.178$ m, $\eta = 0.6$.

$$ D \approx 0.68 \text{m} .$$

It is also important to know where to place the feed. For a direct focus configuration, the focal length is

$$ F = \frac{D^2}{16c} ,$$

where $F$ is the focal length and $c$ is the dish depth (measured from the center to the plane of the rim).

Perhaps the single most important geometric parameter of a dish antenna is the F/D ratio (focal length divided by diameter). This parameter determines the required feed pattern.

To understand this, imagine the feed transmitting a beam toward the dish. The feed’s illumination pattern must match the dish geometry:

  • If the beam is too wide → spillover occurs, and the feed sees warm ground noise, raising the system temperature.
  • If the beam is too narrow → under-illumination reduces gain.

Thus, the feed type is directly tied to the F/D ratio. More details are available in antenna-design references, but they are not covered here.

Example: Discovery Dish (L-band)

Let’s look at an example like the Discovery Dish used for ameature L-band weather satellite reception. The dish diameter is 70 cm and the depth is 12 cm, giving

$$F/D = 0.36 .$$

The half illumination angle is

$$ \theta = \arctan\left(\frac{D/2}{F}\right) = 53.6^\circ ,$$

so the full illumination is

$$ 2\theta \approx 107^\circ .$$

Typically, we want the spillover to be less than 5%, so a feed with a beamwidth of approximately 105 degrees at 1680 MHz is appropriate. This is very wide, so a patch antenna, helical feed or simple PCB feed works well.

However, remember that spillover is often the dominant noise source after LNA noise figure. The sky temperature is roughly 3 K, while the ground is around 280 K, so even modest spillover can add more noise than the LNA itself at L-band.

Sensitivity and System Temperature

For receiveing weak signals, everything comes down to sensitivity:

$$ \text{Sensitivity} = \frac{G}{T_{system}} .$$

$G$ depends on the effective aperture controlled illumination, while $T_{system}$ depends heavily on the spilloever noise and LNA noise figure. Good feed matching, proper illumination, and low spilloever are key to maximizing performance.
