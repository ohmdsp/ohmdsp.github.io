More RFML

A brief tour of more modern methods in radio frequency machine learning


Jul 31, 2026 RF ML RFML Deep Learning Signal Processing

Table of Contents

In an earlier post, I introduced one of the classic entry points into radio frequency machine learning: converting RF data into spectrograms and then applying a convolutional neural network. That workflow is still useful, especially for rapid prototyping and for problems where time-frequency structure is highly informative. But RF machine learning has moved well beyond that first generation of image-style pipelines.

This post is a draft overview of some more modern RFML directions that have become increasingly important: learning directly from I/Q data, self-supervised representation learning, transformer-based sequence models, multimodal fusion, and hybrid methods that combine domain knowledge from signal processing with data-driven learning.

Why RFML is evolving

A lot of early RFML work borrowed ideas from computer vision. That made sense because spectrograms are easy to visualize and CNNs were already proving useful on image tasks. The limitation is that spectrograms are not always the best representation for every RF problem.

When we convert raw complex baseband data into an image, we make several design choices up front:

  1. Window length
  2. FFT size
  3. Overlap
  4. Scaling and normalization
  5. Time-frequency resolution tradeoffs

Those choices can be effective, but they also inject bias and may discard structure that is present in the raw signal. Modern RFML often tries to learn more directly from the data itself or to learn representations that are less tied to a single handcrafted transform.

Learning directly from I/Q samples

One major shift in RFML has been the move toward models that ingest raw or lightly processed complex baseband samples. Instead of treating the signal only as an image, the model can view it as a sequence with amplitude, phase, temporal structure, and correlation patterns that matter for classification, detection, emitter identification, or waveform understanding.

This has several advantages:

  1. It preserves more of the original signal structure.
  2. It can avoid committing too early to a particular time-frequency representation.
  3. It creates opportunities to learn features that are hard to hand engineer.

Of course, this also makes the learning problem harder. Raw I/Q models must learn invariances to time offset, frequency offset, gain variation, noise, channel distortion, and other impairments that a carefully chosen preprocessing pipeline might partly normalize.

Transformers and sequence models for RF data

Another important trend is the use of transformer-style architectures and other sequence models for RF data. Signals are fundamentally structured in time, so it is natural to explore models that can reason across longer temporal contexts than a small convolutional receptive field.

These models are appealing for tasks such as:

  1. Modulation recognition over long bursts
  2. Activity recognition in dense spectrum environments
  3. Protocol and waveform segmentation
  4. Detection of sparse or intermittent emitters
  5. Joint reasoning over long temporal dependencies

The attraction of transformers is not simply that they are fashionable. It is that they offer a framework for learning relationships over time without assuming all relevant structure is local. In RF problems, that can matter when signatures unfold over longer observation windows or when the signal context matters as much as a local pattern.

Self-supervised learning and representation learning

One of the biggest practical issues in RFML is the cost of labeled data. It is usually much easier to collect RF data than it is to label it well. This is especially true when signals overlap, when emitter identities are uncertain, or when the operational environment changes.

That is why self-supervised learning is such an important development. The general idea is to learn a useful representation from large volumes of unlabeled RF data, then fine-tune the resulting model for smaller labeled tasks.

Possible pretext tasks include:

  1. Predicting masked portions of a sequence
  2. Contrasting related and unrelated signal segments
  3. Learning invariance to augmentations such as gain, phase, or frequency shift
  4. Reconstructing corrupted observations

This approach is promising because it aligns well with how RF datasets are often created in practice: lots of raw captures, limited annotation, and changing environments.

Hybrid models that respect signal structure

The most compelling RFML systems are often not purely end-to-end black boxes. In many cases, the best results come from hybrid approaches that combine machine learning with known signal processing structure.

Examples include:

  1. Using synchronization or detection stages before learned classification
  2. Feeding both raw I/Q and spectrogram-like representations into a joint model
  3. Embedding known symmetries or equivariances into the architecture
  4. Using classical estimators to generate features that support a learned back end

This is often a stronger engineering approach than forcing a model to rediscover everything from scratch. RF signals are not arbitrary media. They are generated by physical systems, channel effects, and protocol constraints that we often understand at least partially. Good RFML should make use of that knowledge when it helps.

Robustness matters more than benchmark accuracy

One of the persistent gaps in RFML is the difference between clean benchmark results and field performance. Models may look excellent on curated datasets and then struggle when the deployment environment shifts.

In practice, RFML systems need to deal with:

  1. Mismatched SNR distributions
  2. Unseen emitters or waveforms
  3. Hardware differences across radios
  4. Sampling rate and bandwidth mismatch
  5. Real interference and channel impairments
  6. Limited calibration and uncertain labels

Because of this, modern RFML should be evaluated not only on accuracy, but also on robustness, calibration, domain shift behavior, and the ability to fail gracefully when the model sees something unfamiliar.

Where I think RFML is most interesting

The most interesting direction, in my view, is not simply using larger neural networks on larger RF datasets. It is building methods that connect first principles from signal processing with modern representation learning.

That means asking questions like:

  1. What structure should be learned versus imposed?
  2. When should we use raw I/Q, cyclostationary features, spectrograms, or multiple views together?
  3. How do we encode invariance to nuisance parameters without discarding useful information?
  4. How do we adapt reliably across radios, environments, and collections?
  5. How do we quantify uncertainty when a model encounters a signal class it has never seen before?

Those are engineering questions, not just machine learning questions. That is exactly why RFML remains such a rich area for research and practical development.

Looking ahead

This draft is meant as a starting point for a broader series on more modern RFML methods. Future follow-on posts could go deeper into specific topics such as transformer architectures for I/Q sequences, contrastive learning for RF data, multimodal RF sensing, foundation-model-style pretraining, or practical robustness evaluation for deployed systems.

If nothing else, the main point is this: RFML is no longer only about turning a spectrogram into an image and dropping it into a CNN. The field is increasingly about learned representations, sequential modeling, hybrid signal-processing-aware architectures, and robustness in real operating environments.
