Drone Signals

RF signal technologies of drones


Oct 16, 2025 Digital Communications Drones Modulation

There are ….

DGI:

  1. Lightbridge Used primarily for mid/pro/legacy models. Examples of models that use this technology are: DJI Inspire 2 (Lightbrindge 2), DJI Matrice 100, DJI Matrice 600 (Lightbridge 2).

  2. Wi-Fi or Enhanced Wi-Fi Used in lighter, entry level and early models of DGI drones. Examples of models that use this technology are: DGI Spark, DJI Mavic Mini, DJI Mini SE and DJI Air (original).

  3. Ocusync This is the modern go-to link for DGI drones. Examples of models that use this technology are: DJI Mini 2 (Ocusync 2.0), DJI Mini 3 Pro (Ocusync 3.0), DJI Mavic Pro (Ocusync 1.0), DJI Mavic 2 Pro (Ocusync 2.0), DJI Air 2S (Ocusync 3.0), DJI Mavic 3 (Ocusync 3+).

  4. DJI Digital FPV

  5. Hybrid Ocusync + 4G Cellular A newer dongle for enterprise level products. For certain newer enterprise use cases, DJI offers a Cellular Dongle that lets the drone use 4G as a fallback or augmentation to OcuSync.

AeroScope is DJI’s (formerly) marketed drone monitoring and detection system. Its goal was to allow authorized users (airports, law enforcement, critical infrastructure, etc.) to passively intercept signals emitted by DJI aircraft (i.e. control / telemetry / link data) and thereby obtain identity and flight information about those drones. In effect, the drone is broadcasting (or making available) certain telemetry / identity data (serial number, position, altitude, speed, etc.), which AeroScope receivers decode. There are (or have been) at least two forms:

  1. Stationary / fixed Meant for continuous monitoring over a protected site (airport, prison perimeter, critical facility). According to DJI, under ideal conditions, the stationary version can monitor up to ~50 km
  2. Portable / Mobile A deployable, mobile version (in a case, with battery and omni antennas) for temporary / field use. Range is much shorter (on the order of a few km, e.g. ~5 km or less) depending on environment.

Signal interception & decoding AeroScope listens to the drone ↔ controller link transmissions (radio frequency communications) and demodulates / decodes them to extract embedded telemetry / identity data. The control / telemetry link (e.g. OcuSync, enhanced Wi-Fi) includes packets that contain data like GPS position, serial number, speed, altitude, direction, etc. AeroScope is designed to “tap into” those. Because the AeroScope signal is (or was) by design unencrypted (or only lightly obfuscated / proprietary) in many DJI models, it’s more readily decodable by receivers. The system correlates the RF direction / signal strength / time / multiple antennas to localize the drone and sometimes even the pilot or controller location.

When detection / decoding succeed, AeroScope can display (or make available) the following kinds of data: Drone identity / serial number / model / manufacture info Drone latitude / longitude position, altitude, speed, heading/direction Takeoff location / home point (in some cases) Controller / pilot location (derived via signal direction / triangulation) — though accuracy varies and sometimes fails Flight path / trajectory over time (as the drone moves, it continuously updates) In practical systems, the AeroScope “back end” or management interface collects, logs, and displays these data (maps, alerts, historical logs, integration into security systems, etc.).

DJI has discontinued production of AeroScope units (as of ~2023) amid controversies over use for military targeting in conflicts (e.g. accusations that this system aided targeting of drone operators in warzones).

DJI’s drone-controller links (e.g. OcuSync) use AES-256 encryption, but the “DroneID / AeroScope / Remote ID” broadcasting side has been less straightforward, and there’s ongoing change and controversy around it. In earlier product announcements, DJI explicitly claimed that OcuSync 2.0 communications are encrypted using AES-256 to protect mission data. DJI has announced that, starting in 2023, they are switching AeroScope / DroneID broadcasting to an encrypted protocol, and requiring AeroScope units (or authorized customers) to upgrade their equipment or modules to support the encrypted format.

Express LRS: An open-source, LoRa-based (and FSK variants) link offering very high packet rates (up to 1000 Hz on 2.4 GHz), low latency, excellent range/cost ratio — widely displacing Crossfire in hobby FPV. ELRS is inexpensive (many cheap modules/receivers) and community-driven with fast feature rollout. Want best price / update rate / low latency for freestyle / racing? ExpressLRS on 2.4 GHz with a RadioMaster ELRS radio is hard to beat.

TBS Crossfire: A proprietary long-range FHSS system with CRSF telemetry, battle-tested for long-range FPV and wings. CRSF-style telemetry/packet framing or can interoperate at the message level, so features like LUA telemetry screens, OSD telemetry, and flight-controller integration are straightforward. Crossfire gear (TBS) tends to be pricier but very robust and integrated (TBS antennas, receivers). Want proven long-range & robustness with mature ecosystem and very rugged antennas? Consider TBS Crossfire with TBS receivers and antennas.

Foxeer: Foxeer makes video and imaging hardware (FPV cams, VTX, frames) and some BNF drones; they don’t make a control-link protocol — their gear is commonly paired with Crossfire or ELRS receivers depending on the product/seller.

RadioMaster: Radiomaster makes popular multi-protocol transmitters (TX16S family, etc.) that either come with internal ELRS modules or accept external Crossfire modules in the module bay — i.e., RadioMaster radios let you choose the link ecosystem.

FrSky: FrSky’s signals are not compatible with ExpressLRS, TBS Crossfire, or Spektrum, though they use the same unlicensed bands. RadioMaster and other “multi-protocol” transmitters include firmware modules that can emulate FrSky ACCST/ACCESS frames to talk to FrSky receivers. Its innovation lies in the digital protocol layer—frequency-hopping control, telemetry framing, encryption, and binding logic—not in the physical RF medium itself. Standard RF front-ends (e.g., Semtech / TI transceivers) rather than custom silicon.
