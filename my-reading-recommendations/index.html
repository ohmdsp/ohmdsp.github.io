My Reading Recommendations

More Books


Jun 27, 2024 Books Reading

This is a list of reading suggestions based on what I have found educational and enjoyable. I will continue to update the list as I discover more books worth sharing information about. Warning: I primarily like to read non-fiction.

1. Seven Brief Lessons on Physics, by Carlo Rovelli
This is a well-written book that is both educational and enjoyable. It is a short book, so it can be read in a weekend. I highly recommend it for anyone interested in modern physics.

2. Reality Is Not What It Seems: The Journey to Quantum Gravity, by Carlo Rovelli
I really enjoyed the background and historical context this book provides. Rovelli offers a fresh and engaging take on the evolution of physics, and the historical connections he draws are both enlightening and well presented. The later chapters, which delve into loop quantum gravity, were intriguing but, in my view, less informative than the earlier sections. At one point, there’s an awkward detour into God and religion—a common temptation for physics writers, though arguably beyond their scope of expertise. Overall, this is a compelling and thought-provoking read. It’s the kind of book that’s worth owning and perhaps revisiting more than once.

3. The Signal And The Noise, by Nate Silver
This book is packed with stories and insights about statistical analysis applied to everything from world events and weather patterns to markets, natural disasters, and gambling. It covers a wide range of real-world examples, making it a broad introduction to the role of prediction in modern life. That said, I found the writing a bit too wordy at times and ended up skimming some sections to stay engaged. If you’re looking for a deeper dive into the actual math behind statistical prediction, this isn’t the book for that—but it’s still valuable if you’re interested in how statistical thinking is applied across various domains. The sections on games of chance were particularly compelling.

4. The Theoretical Minimum, by Leonard Susskind
This is a good book for brushing up on your basic physics. I put it in my re-read list since it never hurts to go over it again. I am looking forward to reading and studying Dr. Susskind’s other books on quantum mechanics and relativity. If you have already read Feynman’s Lectures on Physics, then this is a good next step.

5. Thinking Fast and Slow, by Daniel Kahneman
If you’re interested in psychology and the science of human decision-making, this book is well worth your time. It’s not a fast read—it took me several weeks to work through it gradually—but it’s packed with fascinating insights. At times, it can feel like a collection of loosely connected studies and stories rather than a tightly structured narrative. Still, the depth of information about cognitive biases and how we think is incredibly eye-opening. This book will likely have you questioning—and improving—your own decision-making processes.

6. The Radioactive Boy Scout, by Ken Silverstein
I loved this short book! The story is both inspiring and sad. The book presents a lot of general information about radioactivity and physics in an easy-to-read manner. I highly recommend this book to anyone interested in physics or chemistry. It is a great book to share with your teenage son or daughter.

7. Thinking Physics, by Lewis Carroll Epstein
One of the best books ever! You won’t regret owning this book. It’s a brilliant and refreshing approach to learning physics, built not on memorizing formulas but on developing genuine physical intuition. The book presents conceptual puzzles and thought experiments that challenge you to reason through problems the way a physicist does—using logic, visualization, and first-principles thinking rather than rote computation. It’s engaging, playful, and often surprising, encouraging deeper insight into mechanics, energy, relativity, and more. While some explanations assume a bit of prior familiarity with basic physics ideas, the conversational tone and clever illustrations make even sophisticated concepts feel approachable. A highly recommended guide for anyone who wants to sharpen their physical reasoning and learn to think rather than just calculate.

8. Feynman’s Lectures On Physics, by Richard Feynman
I recently revisited these lectures for some self-study. I was reminded how good they are. I will admit, than sometimes the conversations get long and wordy, but Dr. Feynman’s thought process is enlightening. Plan to spend many weeks, maybe months, reading/studying these lectures.

9. Digital Communications, by Andy Bateman
This is meant to be a textbook, but it is short and very well written. I highly recommend this book to any systems engineers looking to get a high-level overview on digital communications. The graphics are also very good.

10. The Perfectionists, by Simon Winchester
A wonderful book about engineering and precision. I really loved the historical aspect of this book along with stories of impressive engineering.

11. Astrophysics for People in a Hurry, by Neil DeGrasse Tyson
An informative quick read perfect for a Sunday afternoon. This book contains simple descriptions of important discoveries and concepts around the Cosmic Background Radiation, Dark Matter, Dark Energy, etc. This would be a good book to share with a young student.

12. Vibrations and Waves in Physics, by Iian G. Main
This book is actually intended as a short textbook for a physics course. However, it is perfect for self-study or refreshing knowlege from your undergraduate physics courses. Keep in mind that a solid understanding of basic physics and differential equations is needed to get the most out of this book.

13. Feynman Lectures On Computation, by Richard Feynman
This is a fantastic book and a great addition to the lectures listed above. Consider this book a complete introduction to logic operations, encoding and the physics of computation. A book worth reading multiple times.

14. The Perfectionists, by Simon Winchester
Although I found the first few chapters of this book to be a little boring and slow, the last several chapters are wonderful and insightful. If you have an interest in the history of navigation, time keeping and how modern scientific measurements have influenced our lives, this book is worth the time to read.

15. The Stars, by H.A.Rey
This book is amazing! Yes, it is written by author of the many popular Curious George books. If you have any interest in the stars and celestial mechanics, you need this book. The drawings and diagrams are incredibly insightful. The sections on the celestial sphere, orbits and the seasons are easy to read and understand. Buy this book!

16. Ultralearning, by James Young
An interesting book with some insightful stories and lessong about people that have mastered a topic or skill thorugh intense effort combined with ability. Like many books written around self imrpovement, some sections are repedetive and too verbose. However, it is not difficult to quickly scan these sections. Worth a quick read if you are interested in learning a something challenging and are not sure how to start.

17. On Tyranny, by Timothy Snyder
This is a short read with some valuable insights and historical context on fascism and authoritarian regimes. Snyder draws from history to highlight patterns of tyranny and offers reflections intended to serve as warnings for modern times. However, the book leans too heavily on comparisons to the 2016 U.S. election and its aftermath, which can feel overly politicized and detracts from the broader historical analysis. When the focus stays on historical examples, the content is engaging and thought-provoking. I recommend this book primarily for its historical perspective and as a starting point for thinking critically about the fragility of democratic institutions.

18. Ice Age, by John and Mary Gribbin
If you are interested in the scientific theories and experiments developed over the past century in order to better understand the long term history of Earth’s climate, then you will enjoy this book.

19. How To Fly A Horse, by Kevin Ashton
A fun book about the history of invention and discovery. Some chapters will be more interesting than others depending on your interests and background.

20. Chasing the Demon, by Dan Hampton
An interesting book about aviation history and the milestones that led up to flying faster than the speed of sound. The book also includes some interesting photographs from the post WWII era when secret flight tests were being made in the US.

21. Rocket Billionaires, by Tim Fernholz
I really enjoyed this book. It offers a well-written and accessible history of the major commercial space companies led by today’s most prominent billionaires. The sections on SpaceX alone make it worth the read. It’s full of behind-the-scenes stories that bring the company’s early struggles and breakthroughs to life. Reading this book actually deepened my interest in the modern commercial space race and the ambitious companies driving it. Highly recommended for anyone curious about the intersection of innovation, business, and space exploration.

22. Zero to One, by Peter Thiel
In my opinion, one of the best startup books out there. Read it!

23. Lost In The Math, by Sabine Hossenfelder
This is a refreshing look at the progress in particle physics. Sabine is very engaging and brings a new perspective to the topic. She also authors a blog and puts out some really educational YouTube videos.

24. Unsettled, by Steven Koonin
This is a thought-provoking book that explores the complexities and uncertainties in climate science. Koonin, a highly qualified physicist with extensive experience in both research and science policy, presents a nuanced look at the challenges of interpreting climate data and models. While the book can feel a bit unorganized at times, the content is compelling and well worth the effort. It encourages readers to think critically about scientific consensus, media narratives, and the intersection of science and policy. Recommended for those looking to better understand the scientific and political landscape surrounding climate change.

25. The Hunter Killers, by Dan Hampton
This is a fascinating book about the Vietnam War and methods used to hunt and destroy SAM missile installations in North Vietnam. Sections of the book are packed with adrenaline infused stories of actual missions. The book has all sorts of interesting information about the strategies and technologies used on both sides. A very enjoyable book!

26. The Operator, by Dan Robert O’Neill
Intense, eye-opening, interesting. Read it!

27. The Way Of The Knife, by Mark Mazzetti
An eye-opening education about how the CIA and DoD conduct war and how it has evolved over the past 25 years. This book contains an impressive amount of information.

28. David and Goliath: Underdogs, Misfits, and the Art of Battling Giants, by Malcolm Gladwell
I know I’m a bit late to this one, but I finally got around to reading it — and I have to admit, it was an engaging and thought-provoking read. The sections on the unintended challenges faced by students at top-tier universities were especially compelling and gave me a lot to think about. While the book is certainly interesting, in my opinion it does occasionally fall into the trap of confirmation bias, shaping stories to fit its central thesis. Even so, it offers plenty of memorable insights and is well worth reading for those interested in psychology, sociology, and the dynamics of advantage and disadvantage.

29. Titan, by Ron Chernow
A very detailed and interesting book about the life of John D. Rockerfeller, Sr. As an entrepreneur it was educational to read about his life and decision processes. It is a thick book, but the account of post civil war industrial development made this book worth heavy cost of time it took to read. There are also many lessons about business and investing worth learning.

30. Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, and the Quest for a Fantastic Future, by Ashlee Vance
This is a compelling book that both excites and disturbs. As an entrepreneur, I found it particularly insightful—it pushed me to think more deeply about what it takes to succeed at truly massive, high-risk ventures. The book doesn’t shy away from the complexities of Musk’s character. Both the admirable traits and the difficult ones are laid bare, offering a well-rounded portrait of a visionary who has reshaped multiple industries. Well worth reading for anyone interested in innovation, leadership, or ambitious entrepreneurship.

31. Einstein: His Life and Universe, by Walter Isaacson
I really enjoyed this biography. In addition to offering accessible explanations of some key physics concepts, it gave me new insight into Einstein as a person. I learned things I hadn’t known before—such as his willingness to admit and publish mistakes, his struggles in personal relationships, and his resistance to the mainstream interpretations of quantum mechanics embraced by other leading physicists. This book paints a fuller, more human picture of Einstein, making it both informative and engaging. Highly recommended for anyone interested in science, history, or the complexity behind great minds.

32. Grant, by Ron Chernow
This is an impressive and deeply informative book. It took me quite a while to get through, but it was absolutely worth the time. I learned a great deal—not just about Ulysses S. Grant himself, but also about the Civil War era in ways I hadn’t fully understood before. I came away with a much greater appreciation for Grant’s character and the immense challenges he faced throughout his life. The book also gave me a deeper understanding of a pivotal and complex period in American history. If you’re interested in U.S. history or strong leadership in times of crisis, I highly recommend reading this book.

33. Steve Jobs, by Walter Isaacson
I read this over the winter months in late 2021 and found it fascinating—especially the parts about Steve Jobs’ youth and family life. His path to success was unconventional, and his approach to life and work was unlike anyone else’s. I especially enjoyed learning about the early days of Apple and the people who played key roles in its founding. While Jobs was clearly a deeply flawed person in many ways, he was undeniably a driving force for innovation. The second half of the book gets a bit long and overly detailed—I’ll admit I skipped a few sections that didn’t hold my interest. Still, it’s a compelling read and well worth the time for anyone interested in technology, creativity, or leadership.

34. The Laws of Human Nature, by Robert Greene
At first, I wasn’t sure how I felt about this book. It seemed like the author was simply criticizing everyone and everything. But a few chapters in, something shifted. I began to find real insight into questions I’ve long had about human behavior—especially in workplace dynamics and interpersonal interactions. The later chapters, which explore how we analyze our own thinking and relationships, were especially impactful. Greene delves into the roots of our habits and conditioning in a way that felt both accessible and profound. This book has genuinely influenced how I think about my work, my goals, and what it means to find fulfillment. Greene reinforces a powerful idea: that challenge and purpose are not just beneficial—they are essential to thriving. I’m glad I stuck with it, and I’m now looking forward to diving into more of Robert Greene’s work.

35. 33 Strategies of War, by Robert Greene
Although I really enjoyed the previous Robert Greene book I read, this one didn’t quite measure up to my expectations. Perhaps I’m simply less interested in the topic, but it felt somewhat forced and less relevant to modern life—especially for anyone seeking a peaceful existence or working in a profession that depends on genuine curiosity and cooperation.

36. Scattered Minds, by Gabor Mate
If you are curious to learn more about attention deficit dissorder, and especially if you are GenX or older, this book is very informative. Since ADHD was not a “thing” when I was growing up, it is hard to understand how it impacts the older generations. This book provided a lot of answers and some compelling ideas.

37. Chip War, by Chris Miller
Chip War by Chris Miller is a fascinating exploration of how semiconductors became the foundation of modern power and progress. The book does an excellent job connecting technological innovation with global politics and economics. While some sections dive deeply into historical and policy details, it remains highly readable and engaging throughout. I especially appreciated how it highlights the fragile and competitive nature of today’s chip supply chain—a topic that feels increasingly relevant to anyone working in technology or engineering.

38. Messy, by Tim Harford
This book offers an insightful look at how disorder, unpredictability, and imperfection can actually foster creativity and innovation. I enjoyed the range of real-world examples showing how flexibility and improvisation often lead to better outcomes than rigid planning. While some chapters felt a bit repetitive, the overall message—that embracing a bit of chaos can make us more effective and adaptable—was both refreshing and thought-provoking. It reminded me of some of my own work styles and preferences.

39. Suprise, Kill, Vanish, by Annie Jacobsen
This was an exciting and somewhat disturbing book to read. It offers a gripping look into the hidden world of covert operations, assassinations, and the moral ambiguity that often accompanies them. Jacobsen’s research is meticulous, and she does an impressive job weaving together history, politics, and human psychology. While some parts are unsettling, the book provides valuable insight into how secret intelligence work has shaped modern geopolitics and national security.

40. Faraday, Maxwell, and the Electromagnetic Field, by Nancy Forbes and Basil Mahon
This book provides an engaging historical look at two of the most important figures in physics and electrical engineering. It does an excellent job explaining how their ideas evolved and ultimately unified electricity and magnetism into a single framework that shaped modern science. However, the pacing felt a bit slow at times, with too much attention given to nonessential details of their personal lives. Still, it’s a worthwhile read for anyone interested in the human story behind some of the most profound discoveries in physics. However, I suggested skimming sections that get boring.

41. Storm in a Teacup, by Helen Czerski
Storm in a Teacup is a delightfully engaging and educational read that brings physics out of the lab and into everyday life. From teacups to teapots, from honey to hurricanes, the book translates fundamental scientific principles into relatable, often playful examples that make complex ideas feel accessible. It strikes a balance between rigor and charm, offering clear explanations without ever feeling dry or academic. The result is a book that not only deepens your understanding of the physical world, but also rekindles a childlike sense of curiosity about the seemingly ordinary phenomena we encounter every day. A fun, insightful journey.

42. Caffeine, by Michael Pollan
This was a very interesting book. Pollan takes a simple, everyday substance and explores its profound impact on human history, productivity, and culture. I enjoyed how he connected personal experience with broader scientific and historical insights. It’s a quick but thought-provoking read that makes you reconsider how something as ordinary as a cup of coffee has shaped modern life and even our patterns of thought.

43. Benjamin Franklin: An American Life, by Walter Isaacson
Benjamin Franklin: An American Life offers a richly detailed and deeply engaging portrait of one of America’s most inventive and influential founders. The biography excels at capturing Franklin’s relentless curiosity—ranging from science and statesmanship to printing and philosophy—and pairs it with an honest exploration of his firm opinions and occasionally controversial stances. While thorough in its historical context, the book never feels dry; instead, it shows Franklin as a dynamic, complex thinker whose curiosity shaped his world and ours. It’s a compelling read for anyone interested in the intersection of intellect, character, and early American history.

44. A Students Guide to Waves, by Daniel Fleisch
I selected this book as the starting point for a comprehensive review of physics that I plan to undertake over the next year. Although I hold a B.Sc. in Physics, most of my career has been in electrical engineering and signal processing, so this proved to be an excellent choice. I was so impressed with the book that I ordered all of the author’s other works. I studied it cover to cover in just a few days and then spent several evenings during the following week working through the chapter problems. The writing is exceptionally clear and concise, and the book does an excellent job of connecting key concepts across different areas of physics.

45. The Forgotten Genius of Oliver Heaviside, by Basil Mahon
A friend handed me this book and suggested that I would enjoy it. It was eye-opening to discover how much Oliver Heaviside contributed to physics and electrical engineering. Much of the modern mathematical form used to describe electricity and magnetism was introduced by him. Though he was somewhat of an outsider to the academic establishment of his time, those with enough humility quickly recognized his genius. I found it fascinating to learn about both the history of his era and the depth of his contributions.

46. The Status Game, by Will Storr
The Status Game is an engaging exploration of one of the most powerful—and often invisible—forces shaping human behavior: our pursuit of status. Storr blends psychology, anthropology, and storytelling to explain how status-seeking underpins everything from social hierarchies to creativity, tribal conflict, and even our sense of personal meaning. The book succeeds in illustrating how deeply status dynamics are woven into daily life, often in ways we’re reluctant to admit. At times, the argument stretches a bit broadly, attributing complex social and historical patterns to status motives more confidently than evidence might fully support. Still, Storr’s narrative is compelling, thought-provoking, and highly readable, offering a useful lens for understanding both individual motivation and the collective behaviors of groups and institutions.

47. The WEIRDest People in the World, by Joseph Henrich
The WEIRDest People in the World offers a fascinating dive into cultural anthropology and provides a compelling framework for understanding how Western psychological and social traits may have emerged. I found the exploration of cultural differences genuinely interesting, particularly the attempt to trace modern Western tendencies to historical shifts in family structure and literacy. That said, much of the book veers into what feels like pseudo-science, leaning heavily on speculative historical causation and tenuous psychological generalizations. At times, the narrative becomes overly detailed and meandering, making arguments that could have been clearer and more persuasive with greater conciseness and restraint. Overall, the concept is intriguing, but the execution left me skeptical and wishing for a more disciplined, evidence-grounded approach.

48. Break Neck, by Dan Wang
Breakneck by Dan Wang is an excellent and insightful analysis of the growing strategic tension between the United States and China. Wang frames the divide not simply in terms of geopolitics or economics, but through the deeper cultural lens of a “lawyerly state” versus an “engineering state,” arguing that the U.S. has prioritized legalism, finance, and abstract systems while China has leaned into production, engineering, and industrial capacity. This perspective offers a refreshing and compelling way to understand each country’s strengths, blind spots, and future trajectories. Highly readable, intellectually grounded, and rich with historical and contemporary examples, the book provides a valuable foundation for anyone trying to make sense of the technological and ideological competition shaping the 21st century.

49. The Radio Operator, by James McGrath Morris
The Radio Operator is a gripping and immersive account that reads at times like a thriller, yet remains firmly rooted in history. James McGrath Morris tells the remarkable true story of Robert W. Ford, a young Royal Air Force veteran who traveled to Tibet in 1950 to help build its first modern radio communications network. What begins as an adventure in a remote and enigmatic land transforms into a high-stakes struggle for survival as the Chinese army invades and Ford becomes one of the last links between Tibet and the outside world. Morris paints Ford with depth and humanity—curious, principled, and quietly courageous—while also portraying the cultural and political crossroads of a nation facing existential threat. This book is both informative and compelling, offering a window into a little-known chapter of history and the pivotal role technology and communication played in it. It’s a story of personal bravery and geopolitical upheaval, and one that is hard to put down.

50. Range: Why Generalists Triumph in a Specialized World, by David J. Epstein Range offers a refreshing case for broad learning in a world obsessed with specialization. David Epstein blends research and real-world examples to show how diverse experiences, cross-disciplinary thinking, and curiosity can fuel creativity and long-term success. While the specialist vs. generalist contrast sometimes feels overstated, the core message is inspiring: cultivating range isn’t lack of focus - it’s a powerful strategy for solving hard problems and thriving in complex environments.

51. Turn the Ship Around!, by David Marquet
An inspiring and thought-provoking book on leadership and organizational culture. Marquet shares his remarkable true story of taking command of a nuclear submarine plagued by poor morale and underperformance, and how he transformed it into the best-performing vessel in the fleet. The key to his success was shifting from a traditional top-down, command-and-control leadership model to one that empowers crew members to lead and take ownership. Rather than making all decisions himself, Marquet taught his team to think critically, take initiative, and embrace responsibility at every level. This book challenges conventional management wisdom and demonstrates that the most effective organizations are built on trust, engagement, and distributed leadership. Highly recommended for anyone interested in leadership, organizational change, and creating cultures where people are motivated to do their best work.

52. Crossing the Chasm, by Geoffrey Moore
Essential reading for anyone involved in technology marketing and product strategy. Moore provides a compelling framework for understanding why many innovative products fail to achieve mainstream adoption despite early success with enthusiasts and early adopters. The core insight is that there exists a significant “chasm” between these early customers and the mainstream market—and most technology companies lack the strategies to bridge it. Moore outlines practical approaches for repositioning products, identifying pragmatist customers, and building the necessary marketing momentum to cross from niche success to mass market adoption. While the book was written in the 1990s and some examples feel dated, the fundamental principles remain highly relevant in today’s tech landscape. This is a must-read for entrepreneurs, product managers, and anyone developing or marketing innovative technology products.

53. Resilience, by Eric Greitens
Written by a Navy SEAL and Rhodes Scholar, this book explores what it means to be resilient and how we can cultivate resilience in our lives. Greitens draws on his military experience, his work in conflict zones, and his study of philosophy to examine how resilient people handle adversity, failure, and loss. Rather than offering simplistic platitudes, the book provides thoughtful, nuanced reflections on how suffering and struggle can become sources of strength and meaning. The narrative weaves together personal stories, historical examples, and philosophical insights that make the concepts accessible and compelling. While the military perspective dominates certain sections, the book’s broader message about purpose, community, and perseverance transcends any single domain. A deeply moving and practically useful book for anyone seeking to understand and strengthen their own resilience.

54. Anything You Want, by Derek Sivers
A short and refreshingly honest book about entrepreneurship and success. Sivers shares insights from his experience building CD Baby and emphasizes that business should be about pursuing what you truly want, not chasing money or metrics. The book’s core message is simple but powerful: make decisions based on your values, and success often follows naturally. Quick read, big impact.

55. Shoe Dog, by Phil Knight
An excellent memoir that reads like a thrilling adventure story. Phil Knight, the co-founder of Nike, shares the remarkable journey from a small startup in 1962 to one of the world’s most iconic brands. The book is remarkably candid, revealing both Knight’s vulnerabilities and his unwavering vision. What makes this book stand out is Knight’s honest reflection on the struggles, near-failures, and tough decisions that shaped Nike. The narrative moves at a brisk pace and captures the essence of what it takes to build something meaningful from nothing. Whether you’re interested in business, entrepreneurship, or simply a compelling personal story, this is a must-read.

56. America’s Bank, by Roger Lowenstein
A fascinating and well-researched history of the creation of the Federal Reserve. Lowenstein chronicles the political intrigue, financial crises, and key personalities that led to the establishment of the Federal Reserve in 1913. The book is engaging and accessible, making complex financial and political history easy to follow. It provides valuable context for understanding how modern banking and monetary policy evolved in the United States. Highly recommended for anyone interested in economic history, finance, or American political history.

57. Principles: Life and Work, by Ray Dalio
Ray Dalio’s memoir and guide to his management philosophy offers some valuable insights into decision-making and organizational culture. The first section detailing his personal journey and early struggles is engaging and motivating. However, the book’s strength is also its weakness—it’s quite long and dense, with large sections that could have been more concise. The principles themselves, while solid, aren’t particularly groundbreaking or unique compared to other business and management books. It’s a worthwhile read if you’re interested in Dalio’s approach to life and work, but it’s not a must-read and may feel repetitive if you’ve already read similar works on management and decision-making.

58. The Lessons of History, by Will and Ariel Durant
A thoughtful exploration of historical patterns and what they reveal about human nature and society. The Durants draw on their vast knowledge of history to identify recurring themes across civilizations and centuries. While the book is informative and offers genuine insights into how history shapes our present, it can be a slow read—the pace is deliberate and sometimes overly philosophical. The ideas are worth engaging with, but you may need to take your time working through it. Recommended for serious history enthusiasts and those interested in historical philosophy.

59. Judging Edward Teller, by Istvan Hargittai
A nuanced biography of Edward Teller, one of physics’ most influential and controversial figures. Hargittai does an excellent job presenting Teller’s life and work with balance, neither demonizing nor idealizing him. The book explores Teller’s contributions to nuclear physics, his role in the Manhattan Project, and the complex moral questions surrounding his later advocacy for nuclear weapons development. It provides valuable context for understanding both the scientific achievements and the ethical dilemmas of the nuclear age. Well-researched and thought-provoking for anyone interested in the history of physics or the intersection of science and policy.

60. General of the Army, by Ed Cray
A comprehensive biography of George C. Marshall, one of the most important military and political figures of the 20th century. The book covers Marshall’s rise through the military ranks, his pivotal role in World War II, and his later career as Secretary of State and Defense. Cray’s research is thorough and detailed, providing deep insight into Marshall’s character and decision-making. That said, the book’s level of detail can feel excessive at times, with sections that could have been more concise without losing the essential narrative. Still, it’s a valuable read for anyone interested in military history, strategic leadership, or the Cold War era.

61. The Power of Habit, by Charles Duhigg
An engaging and eye-opening exploration of how habits work and how they shape our lives, organizations, and society. Duhigg uses compelling stories and scientific research to explain the habit loop—cue, routine, and reward—and demonstrates how understanding this mechanism can help us change our behaviors. The book is accessible and practical, offering real-world examples ranging from personal habits to organizational change. Whether you’re looking to break bad habits or build good ones, this book provides valuable insights and actionable strategies. A thought-provoking read that will make you more aware of the habits you engage in daily.

62. Political Risk, by Condoleezza Rice
Unfortunately, this book did not live up to my expectations. While Rice’s credentials as former Secretary of State and her deep experience in foreign policy and geopolitics should have made for compelling insights, the book falls short of being engaging. The analysis, while competent, comes across as somewhat dry and academic. The book covers important topics—political instability, governance, and international relations—but the presentation lacks the narrative energy or fresh perspective that might make it truly compelling. If you’re looking for a rigorous academic treatment of political risk from a distinguished author, this may have value, but it’s not a page-turner and doesn’t offer particularly novel insights for those already familiar with international affairs.

63. The Radium Girls, by Kate Moore
A powerful and deeply moving account of the young women who worked in radium dial factories, painting luminous watch faces with brushes moistened in radioactive paint. Moore tells their tragic true story with both heart and rigor, documenting how these women unknowingly poisoned themselves in pursuit of good, honest work. What makes the book so compelling is how it captures the human cost of ignorance and corporate negligence—these were dedicated workers trying to do the right thing, yet their efforts led to devastating health consequences because the dangers of radium were either unknown or deliberately concealed. The book also chronicles their remarkable fight for justice and recognition. It’s an important historical account that is both inspiring and heartbreaking. Highly recommended for anyone interested in labor history, social justice, or the ethics of industrial safety.

64. The Silk Roads, by Peter Frankopan
An expansive and illuminating history that reframes world history from the perspective of the Silk Roads rather than traditional Western-centric narratives. Frankopan traces the interconnected networks of trade, culture, and conflict that linked Asia, the Middle East, Europe, and beyond across centuries. The book is ambitious in scope and rich with fascinating stories of merchants, empires, religions, and ideas traveling these routes. However, its breadth can sometimes feel overwhelming, and some readers may find it jumps between regions and time periods too quickly for easy digestion. Still, it’s a thought-provoking and valuable perspective that challenges conventional historical narratives and reveals how deeply interconnected civilizations have always been. Recommended for history enthusiasts seeking a fresh and global perspective.

65. An Introduction to Information Theory, by John Pierce
A classic and accessible introduction to the mathematical foundations of information theory. Pierce does an excellent job making complex concepts clear and intuitive, using numerous examples and illustrations to ground abstract ideas in practical applications. The book covers entropy, coding, channel capacity, and other key information theory concepts in a way that’s both rigorous and readable. I found it valuable enough to read twice, as revisiting it deepened my understanding of how information is fundamentally quantified and transmitted. Highly recommended for anyone interested in communications, signal processing, or the theoretical underpinnings of modern information systems.

66. The World’s Religions, by Huston Smith
A comprehensive reference guide to the world’s major religions, covering Christianity, Islam, Judaism, Hinduism, Buddhism, Confucianism, Taoism, and others. Smith provides clear, respectful, and informative overviews of each tradition’s core beliefs, practices, and history. The book is exceptionally well-written and educational for learning about different faiths. However, it’s primarily a reference book rather than a narrative you’ll want to read straight through from cover to cover. It’s best approached as a resource to consult when you want to understand a particular religion’s fundamentals or compare different traditions. Highly useful for anyone seeking a balanced and accessible introduction to world religions.

67. Quanta and Fields, by Sean Carroll
A solid introduction to quantum field theory presented in an accessible manner. Carroll does a commendable job explaining complex theoretical concepts without requiring advanced mathematical background. The book covers key ideas in quantum mechanics and field theory, making it valuable for anyone wanting to understand the theoretical foundations of modern physics. While it’s a good read and well-written, it’s not quite my favorite among Carroll’s works. Some sections could have been more engaging or deeper in their exploration. Still, it’s a worthwhile book for physics enthusiasts looking to grasp the fundamentals of quantum field theory.

68. Meditations, by Marcus Aurelius
A timeless masterpiece of Stoic philosophy that has only grown richer for me over the past decade. When I first read it, I appreciated it as a collection of personal reflections from a Roman emperor. But with repeated reading and life experience, I’ve discovered there’s far more depth here than I initially recognized. Marcus Aurelius grapples with fundamental questions about virtue, duty, mortality, and how to live a meaningful life—ideas that feel as relevant today as they were nearly 2,000 years ago. The book is best read slowly and thoughtfully, returning to it over time as your own life experience deepens your understanding. It’s a book worth owning and revisiting throughout your life. Highly recommended for anyone seeking wisdom on how to navigate life’s challenges with grace and purpose.
