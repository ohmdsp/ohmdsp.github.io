From Vision to Prototype

A Mentoring Approach for Engineering Students


Nov 12, 2025 Mentoring Teaching Engineering

This post describes my teaching and mentoring approach that has evolved over the past decade through mentoring new engineering hires and summer interns. The approach emphasizes system-level thinking, project-driven learning, and just-in-time introduction of theoretical principles, with the aim of bridging the gap between classroom instruction and the realities of engineering practice.

The method unfolds in four stages: establishing a vision, decomposing into subsystems, cycling between systems and principles, and validating through demonstration.

Start with a Vision

Learning begins with an ambitious project vision that establishes motivation and orients students/interns toward a “system-first” perspective. A compelling vision provides a unifying anchor that makes learning more meaningful.

For example, a motivating vision might be: “Let’s build a radar payload for a drone and use it to detect moving vehicles, drones, and people.” This type of challenge immediately connects to real-world systems and sparks curiosity.

By contrast, a limited goal such as “Let’s simulate a radar signal and then write a program to compute and plot a spectrogram” may build technical skill but risks feeling abstract and disconnected. Smaller exercises have value, but their meaning is magnified when embedded within a larger challenge.

Decompose into Subsystems

Large projects naturally break into subsystems, each of which serves as an entry point for targeted instruction. When students encounter barriers, mentors can introduce the relevant first principles, ensuring that theory is tied to larger challenges.

For instance, a radar payload for a drone can be divided into:

  • RF front-end design and antennas
  • Waveform design and selection
  • Digital signal processing for detection and tracking
  • Embedded computing and control
  • Power management and integration with the airframe
  • Data links and communications
  • Test, calibration, and validation procedures

Each subsystem provides opportunities to zoom in on fundamentals while maintaining momentum toward the overall system vision.

Iterative Cycle of Action

The approach follows a cycle that alternates between system-level challenges and principle-level instruction. Students first encounter a system obstacle, then learn the underlying principles needed to address it, integrate the solution, and return to the system view where new challenges emerge.

In this way, principles appear as direct answers to student-driven questions, making them more relevant and memorable.

Demonstration and Validation

Regular integration, testing, and demonstration are essential. Demonstrations expose weaknesses, foster resilience, and reinforce collaboration. They also provide milestones that build confidence and accountability, showing students that their work has tangible impact.

Learning Stages and Benefits

The method cultivates growth across several dimensions:

  • Immersion — Engagement comes from pursuing a coherent project vision rather than isolated tasks.
  • Integration — Students acquire practical skills aligned with professional expectations.
  • Authenticity — Projects mirror the realities of engineering practice.
  • Resilience — Students learn to face ambiguity and resolve challenges through iteration.
  • Confidence — Students develop the ability to confront and solve complex, open-ended problems.

Conclusion

Not every course or program is suited to this model. Foundational instruction in mathematics, physics, and core engineering theory remains indispensable and is often best delivered through traditional approaches. However, when students have opportunities to engage in courses centered on ambitious, system-level projects—ideally not only in a senior capstone but earlier in their studies — they experience a context that more closely resembles professional engineering practice.

Such experiences can help students and interns develop confidence in applying theory to open-ended problems and strengthen their motivation to learn. They also create natural bridges between academic learning and real-world engineering challenges. For departments, this approach may encourage greater student engagement, contributing over time to a richer leerning environment.
